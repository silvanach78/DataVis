Module 7: Dashboards for Managerial Decision-Making

Introduction

Organizations increasingly rely on dashboards to monitor performance, track progress toward strategic goals, and support data-driven decision making. Advances in data systems and visualization tools have made it possible for managers to access real-time performance information across many areas of the organization.1Few, S. (2013). Information dashboard design: Displaying data for at-a-glance monitoring (2nd ed.). Analytics Press.

In simple terms, a dashboard can be defined as a way of displaying various types of visual data in one place. Dashboards bring together multiple metrics and visualizations into a single interface, allowing managers to quickly assess whether performance is meeting expectations and identify areas that may require attention. However, simply displaying many charts on one screen does not guarantee that a dashboard will support effective decision making. Poorly designed dashboards can overwhelm users with excessive information, obscure key patterns, or emphasize metrics that are not directly relevant to organizational objectives.1Few, S. (2013). Information dashboard design: Displaying data for at-a-glance monitoring (2nd ed.). Analytics Press.

For managers, the value of a dashboard lies not in the volume of information it displays but in its ability to highlight the most important indicators of organizational performance. Effective dashboards provide a clear overview of performance trends, enable comparisons across units or time periods, and support timely decisions when performance deviates from expectations.2Tableau. (n.d.). What is a KPI dashboard?

In this module, we examine how dashboards support managerial decision making and explore principles that help ensure dashboards communicate performance information clearly and effectively.

The Role of Dashboards in Organizations

Dashboards are visual interfaces that present key performance indicators and other relevant metrics in a concise and easily interpretable format.1Few, S. (2013). Information dashboard design: Displaying data for at-a-glance monitoring (2nd ed.). Analytics Press. Managers commonly use dashboards to:

  • monitor operational performance
  • track progress toward strategic goals
  • identify emerging problems or risks
  • compare performance across teams, products, or regions

Because dashboards often display multiple metrics simultaneously, they provide a high-level overview of organizational performance. For example, a marketing dashboard might include metrics such as:

  • website traffic
  • conversion rates
  • customer acquisition costs
  • campaign performance

A supply chain dashboard might track:

  • inventory levels
  • delivery times
  • supplier performance
  • production efficiency

In both cases, the dashboard enables managers to quickly assess whether performance is improving, declining, or remaining stable. Dashboards therefore function as decision-support tools, helping managers focus attention on areas that may require further investigation or action.2Tableau. (n.d.). What is a KPI dashboard?

Monitoring vs Decision Dashboards

Dashboards are not inherently different types of tools, but they can be used in different ways depending on managerial needs. In practice, the same dashboard may support both monitoring and decision-making activities.

When used for monitoring, dashboards rely heavily on preattentive processing to allow managers to quickly detect patterns, trends, and deviations from expected performance. Through effective visual hierarchy and simplified design, managers can identify signals with minimal cognitive effort. When used for decision-making, those same visualizations require deeper interpretation, where managers move beyond detection to evaluate evidence, assess implications, and determine appropriate actions.

Rather than representing separate categories, monitoring and decision-making reflect different levels of engagement with the same information. Monitoring answers the question “Is something changing or off track?”, while decision-making addresses “What should we do about it?”. In this way, monitoring often serves as a trigger for deeper analysis and action.

Monitoring Dashboards

Monitoring dashboards track key metrics over time and help managers detect deviations from expected performance.1Few, S. (2013). Information dashboard design: Displaying data for at-a-glance monitoring (2nd ed.). Analytics Press. These dashboards are designed to support rapid, at-a-glance interpretation.

Monitoring dashboards typically display:

  • historical trends
  • current performance levels
  • comparisons against targets or benchmarks

Their primary purpose is to answer questions such as:

  • Are we meeting our performance targets?
  • Are any indicators showing unusual changes?
  • Do we need to investigate potential problems?

Because they rely on quick pattern recognition, monitoring dashboards are closely aligned with principles from earlier modules, such as preattentive attributes and visual hierarchy, which reduce cognitive load and allow managers to detect meaningful signals efficiently. However, while monitoring dashboards are effective at identifying when attention is needed, they do not by themselves explain why a change has occurred or what action should be taken.

Decision Dashboards

Decision dashboards support managerial evaluation and action by organizing information in ways that facilitate comparison, trade-off analysis, and interpretation.1Few, S. (2013). Information dashboard design: Displaying data for at-a-glance monitoring (2nd ed.). Analytics Press. Rather than simply displaying performance metrics, these dashboards help managers construct evidence-based requests by linking data to decisions.

In this context, dashboards provide the evidence component of the decision-making process described in Module 6. Managers must then interpret that evidence to form a claim, evaluate its implications for organizational performance, and determine an appropriate recommendation.

For example, a pricing dashboard might display:

  • sales volume by price level
  • profit margins across products
  • demand sensitivity to price changes

These visualizations allow managers to compare alternatives and evaluate potential outcomes. Unlike monitoring, which emphasizes rapid detection, decision-oriented use of dashboards involves deeper analytical processing, where managers integrate multiple indicators, assess trade-offs, and justify action.

In practice, decision-making often builds directly on monitoring. Once a dashboard signals a deviation from expectations, managers use the same or related visualizations to investigate causes, evaluate options, and develop evidence-based recommendations.

Designing Dashboards for Managers

Effective dashboards translate complex data into visual structures that align with how humans perceive information and make decisions. Because dashboards are used by decision makers who often face significant time constraints, effective design is essential. Dashboard design should not be viewed simply as a visual task, but as a way to support how managers perceive, interpret, and act on information. The principles below ensure that dashboards reduce cognitive load, highlight meaningful signals, and support evidence-based decision making.

Focus on Key Performance Indicators

Dashboards should emphasize metrics that directly reflect organizational objectives.2Tableau. (n.d.). What is a KPI dashboard? Key Performance Indicators (KPIs) represent measurable outcomes used to assess progress toward strategic goals.

Examples include:

  • revenue growth
  • profit margins
  • customer retention
  • production efficiency
  • employee turnover

From the perspective of evidence-based decision making (see Module 6), KPIs function as organizational warrants. A warrant represents the shared understanding of what constitutes success within the organization. It answers the critical managerial question: “Why does this evidence matter?”

For example, if a dashboard shows a decrease in customer churn, the underlying warrant is that reducing churn is important because it improves long-term profitability. Without a clear warrant, a manager may observe a change in a metric but be unable to determine whether it justifies action.

By aligning dashboards with KPIs, organizations ensure that the evidence presented in visualizations can be meaningfully connected to claims, implications, and ultimately recommendations. In this way, KPIs transform raw data into strategically relevant information that supports decision making.

Including too many metrics reduces the signal-to-noise ratio, making it more difficult to identify meaningful patterns and weakening the effectiveness of the dashboard.1Few, S. (2013). Information dashboard design: Displaying data for at-a-glance monitoring (2nd ed.). Analytics Press.

Organize Information Clearly

The layout of a dashboard should guide the viewer’s attention toward the most important information. Effective dashboards use visual hierarchy to emphasize key metrics, placing the most important indicators in prominent positions such as the top or center of the display.3Few, S. (2012). Show me the numbers: Designing tables and graphs to enlighten (2nd ed.). Analytics Press.

Grouping related metrics together improves comprehension by allowing managers to interpret relationships between indicators more easily. These design choices build on principles from earlier modules, where visual hierarchy and spatial organization help reduce cognitive load and support faster, more accurate interpretation.

Use Appropriate Visualization Types

The choice of visualization should match the type of information being presented.4Yau, N. (2013). Data points: Visualization that means something. Wiley. Selecting appropriate visual encodings allows managers to leverage preattentive processing to quickly detect patterns and differences.

For example:

  • line charts work well for showing trends over time
  • bar charts are useful for comparing categories
  • bullet charts are effective for comparing performance against targets

Using consistent chart types across the dashboard improves readability and reduces the cognitive effort required to interpret information.3Few, S. (2012). Show me the numbers: Designing tables and graphs to enlighten (2nd ed.). Analytics Press. Poorly chosen visualizations can obscure patterns and make it more difficult to construct accurate interpretations.

Avoid Visual Clutter

Because dashboards often contain multiple charts and indicators, excessive visual elements can quickly overwhelm the viewer.1Few, S. (2013). Information dashboard design: Displaying data for at-a-glance monitoring (2nd ed.). Analytics Press. Visual clutter increases cognitive load and makes it more difficult to distinguish meaningful signals from noise.

Common sources of clutter include:

  • unnecessary gridlines
  • excessive colors
  • decorative graphics
  • redundant labels

Reducing unnecessary elements improves clarity and ensures that the most important information stands out. This aligns with earlier principles related to signal-to-noise ratio and supports more effective monitoring and decision-making.

Ensure Accurate and Focused Communication

Dashboards must not only present information clearly, but also ensure that the information is accurate, trustworthy, and meaningful. Because dashboards often support managerial decisions, misleading or poorly constructed visualizations can lead to ineffective or costly outcomes.

From the perspective of Module 4, dashboards should communicate data ethically and accurately. This includes avoiding misleading design choices, such as distorted axes or inappropriate comparisons, and ensuring that uncertainty is appropriately represented when relevant. When variability, incomplete data, or estimation is involved, dashboards should provide sufficient context to prevent overconfidence in the results.

At the same time, dashboards should reflect principles from Module 5 related to storytelling and focus. Effective dashboards do not attempt to show everything. Instead, they present a carefully selected set of visualizations that together communicate a clear and coherent message.

Including too many charts or metrics can overwhelm decision makers and obscure the most important insights. Each visualization should contribute to answering a meaningful managerial question or supporting an evidence-based recommendation. In this way, dashboards function not only as reporting tools, but as structured narratives that guide interpretation and decision making.

By combining accurate representation of data with a focused and intentional design, dashboards help ensure that managers can both trust the information presented and understand its implications for action.

Instructor Note. The principles in this section build directly on concepts introduced in earlier modules. Visual hierarchy and preattentive processing (Modules 2–3) explain how dashboards support rapid perception, while principles of ethical and accurate data representation (Module 4) ensure that the information can be trusted. Storytelling and focus (Module 5) guide the selection and organization of visualizations so that dashboards communicate a clear message, and the claim → evidence → implication → recommendation framework (Module 6) explains how managers use that information to make decisions. In this sense, an effective dashboard is not defined by how much information it contains, but by how effectively it supports accurate interpretation and informed decision making.

Interpreting Dashboards as a Manager

Managers should approach dashboards as tools for identifying patterns and guiding further inquiry, rather than as complete explanations of organizational performance.2Tableau. (n.d.). What is a KPI dashboard? While dashboards make patterns visible, effective decision making requires interpreting those patterns within a broader analytical framework.

From the perspective of evidence-based decision making (Module 6), dashboards provide the evidence that managers use to construct claims, evaluate implications, and determine appropriate recommendations. Interpreting a dashboard therefore involves moving beyond observation to structured reasoning.

When reviewing a dashboard, managers may ask questions such as:

  • Which metrics show significant changes?
  • Are any indicators deviating from their targets?
  • Do these changes align with our key performance indicators (warrants)?
  • What are the potential implications for organizational performance?
  • What actions, if any, should be considered?

Dashboards often serve as starting points for deeper analysis. When a metric signals a potential issue, managers should evaluate whether the evidence is sufficient, consider sources of uncertainty, and determine whether additional analysis is needed before taking action.

By combining pattern recognition with structured reasoning, dashboards support more informed and proactive managerial decision making.2Tableau. (n.d.). What is a KPI dashboard?

Best Practices for Managerial Dashboards

  • Focus on a limited number of meaningful metrics aligned with organizational objectives (KPIs).
  • Design visualizations to support rapid perception using visual hierarchy and preattentive attributes.
  • Ensure that data is represented accurately and ethically, and that uncertainty is communicated when relevant.
  • Use dashboards to communicate a clear and focused message rather than displaying all available data.
  • Provide context for interpreting performance, such as benchmarks, targets, or historical comparisons.

Dashboards should support inquiry and discussion rather than simply presenting static information.1Few, S. (2013). Information dashboard design: Displaying data for at-a-glance monitoring (2nd ed.). Analytics Press. When dashboards prompt managers to ask questions and investigate underlying patterns, they become valuable tools for continuous improvement.

Effective dashboards integrate principles from across the course. They leverage perceptual principles to make patterns visible (Modules 2–3), ensure that information is accurate and trustworthy (Module 4), communicate insights clearly and concisely (Module 5), and support structured decision making (Module 6).

By using dashboards thoughtfully, managers can transform complex data into actionable insights that support informed decision making.4Yau, N. (2013). Data points: Visualization that means something. Wiley. In this way, dashboards do not replace managerial judgment; they enhance it by making relevant information visible, interpretable, and actionable.

Key Takeaways

  • Dashboards provide managers with a visual interface for monitoring organizational performance and supporting decision making.
  • The same dashboard can support both monitoring and decision-making, depending on how managers interpret and use the information.
  • Effective dashboards focus on Key Performance Indicators (KPIs), which function as organizational warrants that define what constitutes success.
  • Visual hierarchy and preattentive attributes (Modules 2–3) enable rapid detection of patterns and deviations.
  • Accurate and ethical data representation (Module 4) ensures that dashboard information can be trusted and that uncertainty is appropriately considered.
  • Storytelling and focus (Module 5) ensure that dashboards communicate a clear and meaningful message rather than overwhelming the viewer.
  • Dashboards provide the evidence that supports structured decision making through the claim → evidence → implication → recommendation framework (Module 6).
  • Managers should use dashboards not as final answers, but as tools for identifying patterns, guiding inquiry, and supporting evidence-based decisions.

Managerial Insight

Managers today operate in environments where decisions must often be made quickly and under conditions of uncertainty. Dashboards help address this challenge by summarizing large amounts of information into a format that can be interpreted rapidly.1Few, S. (2013). Information dashboard design: Displaying data for at-a-glance monitoring (2nd ed.). Analytics Press.

However, effective managers do not treat dashboards as answers. Instead, they use dashboards as inputs into a broader decision-making process. Visualizations provide evidence, but managers must interpret that evidence in relation to organizational goals (warrants), evaluate its implications, and determine appropriate actions.

This requires not only the ability to recognize patterns, but also the ability to question them. Managers must consider whether the data is accurate, whether uncertainty may affect interpretation, and whether the dashboard highlights the most relevant information or obscures important context.

In this way, dashboards become more than reporting tools. They become platforms that support continuous monitoring, critical thinking, and evidence-based decision making. When used effectively, dashboards help managers move from simply observing performance to understanding its implications and acting on it strategically.
