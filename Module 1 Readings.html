Module 1: Data Visualization and Managerial Decisions

Introduction

Managers rarely suffer from a lack of data. Instead, they struggle with interpreting complex information quickly enough to make effective decisions. Modern organizations generate enormous volumes of data, from sales transactions and marketing analytics to operational metrics and customer feedback. While this information has the potential to improve decision making, it can also overwhelm decision makers if it is poorly communicated.8Streeb, D., El-Assady, M., Keim, D. A., & Chen, M. (2021). Why visualize? Arguments for visual support in decision making. IEEE Computer Graphics and Applications, 41(2), 17–22.

Data visualization plays a crucial role in transforming raw data into meaningful insights. By representing data visually through charts, dashboards, and other graphical formats, visualization allows managers to detect patterns, trends, and anomalies more quickly than they often could from tables or reports alone. When used effectively, visualization does not merely present information; it supports managerial judgment and helps guide action.4Healy, K. (2018). Data visualization: A practical introduction. Princeton University Press.5Midway, S. R. (2020). Principles of effective data visualization. Patterns, 1(9), 100141.

In this course, we approach data visualization not as a technical skill for analysts but as a communication tool for managers. The goal is not to create visually complex charts, but to design visuals that help decision makers answer one of the most important questions in management: What should we do next?1Berinato, S. (2016). Visualizations that really work. Harvard Business Review.2Few, S. (2012). Show me the numbers: Designing tables and graphs to enlighten (2nd ed.). Analytics Press.

A Brief History of Data Visualization

Although visualization has become increasingly important in modern business analytics, it is not a new concept. Humans have long used visual representations to explain complex observations.9Tufte, E. (2001). The visual display of quantitative information (2nd ed.). Graphics Press.

One early example appears in the work of astronomers studying sunspots in the early 17th century. Observations like Christoph Scheiner’s 1626 diagrams of sunspot movement used sequential drawings to show how spots appeared to move across the surface of the sun over time (Figure 1-1). These visual records allowed scientists to identify patterns that could not be easily explained through written description alone.7Scheiner, C. (1630). Rosa ursina sive Sol ex admirando facularum & macularum suarum phoenomeno varius. National Library of Naples Collection.

Note: Scheiner's diagram makes it evident that revealing patterns has been a key characteristic of data visualization since its early stages. From Scheiner, C. Another historical example is the 1644 diagram by Michael Florent van Langren, which compared different scholars’ estimates of the longitudinal distance between Toledo and Rome (Figure 1-2). By plotting the estimates along a line, van Langren made disagreement among experts immediately visible. The visualization transformed a long list of numbers into a clear depiction of uncertainty and variation.3Friendly, M., & Denis, D. (2005). The early origins and development of the scatterplot. During the late 19th and early 20th centuries, graphical methods became more common in scientific research and textbooks as statistical thinking expanded across disciplines. Charts and diagrams began appearing more frequently in academic publications and government reports. According to scholars, the 20th century developments that accelerated this trend are:3Friendly, M., & Denis, D. (2005). The early origins and development of the scatterplot.   • The rise of formal statistical methods
  • The development of computers capable of large-scale calculations
  • Improvements in digital displays and interactive dashboards

Today, data visualization has become an essential component of data-driven decision making. At the same time, the explosion of data across industries, from finance to marketing to operations, means that organizations increasingly face a new challenge: how to extract meaning from massive amounts of data and information.8Streeb, D., El-Assady, M., Keim, D. Evidence-Based Management

Modern management increasingly emphasizes evidence-based decision making. Evidence-based management refers to the practice of using the best available information, including data, analytics, professional expertise, and organizational context, to guide decisions.8Streeb, D., El-Assady, M., Keim, D. A., & Chen, M. (2021). Why visualize? Arguments for visual support in decision making. IEEE Computer Graphics and Applications, 41(2), 17–22.

Rather than relying solely on intuition or tradition, evidence-based managers ask questions such as:

  • What does the data actually show?
  • What patterns or trends are present?
  • What evidence supports a particular course of action?

Visualization plays a critical role in this process because it helps decision makers interpret evidence quickly and accurately. However, evidence alone does not guarantee good decisions. Data must be interpreted, contextualized, and communicated effectively. Poorly designed charts can mislead decision makers or obscure important insights.5Midway, S. R. (2020). Principles of effective data visualization. Patterns, 1(9), 100141.8Streeb, D., El-Assady, M., Keim, D. A., & Chen, M. (2021). Why visualize? Arguments for visual support in decision making. IEEE Computer Graphics and Applications, 41(2), 17–22.

Therefore, the role of visualization in evidence-based management is not simply to display numbers but to clarify the implications of those numbers. To that end, in the following sections, we review the process of transforming data into insights that support decisions through data visualization.2Few, S. (2012). Show me the numbers: Designing tables and graphs to enlighten (2nd ed.). Analytics Press.

Data, Information, and Managerial Insight

A common misunderstanding in organizations is the belief that more data automatically leads to better decisions. In reality, effective decision making depends on moving through three stages:10Tukey, J. W., & Wilk, M. B. (1966). Data analysis and statistics: An expository overview. In Proceedings of the November 7–10, 1966, fall joint computer conference (pp. 695–709).

Data - Raw observations or measurements.

  • Sales transactions
  • Website visits
  • Production output
  • Survey responses

Data by itself does not necessarily reveal meaning.

Information - Data that has been organized or summarized.

  • Monthly sales totals
  • Average customer satisfaction scores
  • Conversion rates

Information provides structure but still requires interpretation.

Managerial Insight - Understanding that leads to actionable conclusions.

  • Identifying a declining sales trend in a key market
  • Recognizing that a marketing campaign improved conversion rates
  • Detecting operational inefficiencies in production

Data analysis and visualization play a key role in this transformation. A well-designed chart can reveal relationships that might remain hidden in spreadsheets or reports. Ultimately, managers do not need raw data or even summarized information. They need insight that supports decisions.4Healy, K. (2018). Data visualization: A practical introduction. Princeton University Press.5Midway, S. R. (2020). Principles of effective data visualization. Patterns, 1(9), 100141.

From Insight to Recommendation

In managerial contexts, visualization rarely represents the final step of analysis. Instead, it serves as a bridge between data and action. A useful framework for thinking about visualization in decision-making contexts is: Data → Information → Insight → Recommendation.1Berinato, S. (2016). Visualizations that really work. Harvard Business Review.8Streeb, D., El-Assady, M., Keim, D. A., & Chen, M. (2021). Why visualize? Arguments for visual support in decision making. IEEE Computer Graphics and Applications, 41(2), 17–22.

A recommendation is a proposed action based on the insight. For example:

  • If the managerial insight you extract from analyzing data is: "Sales have decreased in the Northeast region."
  • An appropriate recommendation may be "Increase targeted marketing efforts in the Northeast region."

Without a clear recommendation, even the most sophisticated visualization may fail to influence decisions.1Berinato, S. (2016). Visualizations that really work. Harvard Business Review.6Nussbaumer Knaflic, C. (2015). Storytelling with data: A data visualization guide for business professionals. Wiley.

Decision Relevance vs. Analytical Depth

One challenge in managerial communication is balancing analytical complexity with decision relevance. Data analysts often focus on technical precision, detailed models, and statistical rigor. While these elements are important, they can overwhelm decision makers who must evaluate information quickly. Managers typically care most about three questions:2Few, S. (2012). Show me the numbers: Designing tables and graphs to enlighten (2nd ed.). Analytics Press.

  • What is happening?
  • Why is it happening?
  • What should we do about it?

Effective visualizations prioritize these questions by highlighting the most relevant information rather than displaying every available detail. As Stephen Few and other visualization researchers emphasize, visual communication should focus on clarity, simplicity, and relevance rather than decorative complexity.2Few, S. (2012). Show me the numbers: Designing tables and graphs to enlighten (2nd ed.). Analytics Press.5Midway, S. R. (2020). Principles of effective data visualization. Patterns, 1(9), 100141.

Cognitive Load and Executive Attention

Human attention is limited. Executives often review multiple reports, dashboards, and presentations within a short period of time. When visualizations contain unnecessary elements, such as excessive colors, labels, or decorative features, they increase cognitive load.5Midway, S. R. (2020). Principles of effective data visualization. Patterns, 1(9), 100141.

Cognitive load refers to the amount of mental effort required to process information. When cognitive load is high, decision makers may struggle to interpret the data correctly. Good visualizations reduce cognitive load by:5Midway, S. R. (2020). Principles of effective data visualization. Patterns, 1(9), 100141.

  • Removing unnecessary visual elements
  • Highlighting key patterns
  • Guiding attention to the most important information

Research in information visualization and psychology shows that humans process visual patterns much faster than numerical tables. Simple visual structures (e.g., aligned bars or trends over time) allow viewers to quickly identify relationships.4Healy, K. (2018). Data visualization: A practical introduction. Princeton University Press.8Streeb, D., El-Assady, M., Keim, D. A., & Chen, M. (2021). Why visualize? Arguments for visual support in decision making. IEEE Computer Graphics and Applications, 41(2), 17–22.

This is why decluttering visualizations is so important. As Cole Nussbaumer Knaflic emphasizes, many default chart settings in software tools introduce visual noise that distracts from the underlying message. Removing these elements helps ensure that the audience focuses on the data itself rather than the chart formatting.2Few, S. (2012). Show me the numbers: Designing tables and graphs to enlighten (2nd ed.). Analytics Press.6Nussbaumer Knaflic, C. (2015). Storytelling with data: A data visualization guide for business professionals. Wiley.

The Role of Storytelling in Managerial Communication

Effective decision communication often requires more than presenting charts. Managers must also explain the significance of the data and guide the audience toward a conclusion. This is where storytelling becomes important. Data storytelling involves organizing analysis into a narrative that helps the audience understand:1Berinato, S. (2016). Visualizations that really work. Harvard Business Review.6Nussbaumer Knaflic, C. (2015). Storytelling with data: A data visualization guide for business professionals. Wiley.

  • the context of the problem
  • the evidence supporting the insight
  • the recommended course of action

According to Storytelling with Data, effective communication begins by clearly defining the audience and the message before building visualizations.6Nussbaumer Knaflic, C. (2015). Storytelling with data: A data visualization guide for business professionals. Wiley.

Identifying the “Big Idea” and structuring communication through tools such as storyboarding helps ensure that visualizations serve a clear purpose rather than simply presenting information. In practice, this means asking questions such as:6Nussbaumer Knaflic, C. (2015). Storytelling with data: A data visualization guide for business professionals. Wiley.

  • Who is the audience?
  • What decision are they trying to make?
  • What information do they need to make that decision?

By answering these questions before creating visualizations, we can ensure that visualizations support the intended message rather than distracting from it.1Berinato, S. (2016). Visualizations that really work. Harvard Business Review.6Nussbaumer Knaflic, C. (2015). Storytelling with data: A data visualization guide for business professionals. Wiley.

Practical Example: From Data to Insight to Managerial Decision

Suppose you work for a startup that manufactures window-cleaning drones. As part of a performance review, you are examining cost and profitability data across five drone models. The visualization turns raw data into more meaningful information: the average profit margin is higher when testing is performed by experienced QA technicians than when it is performed by junior technicians.8Streeb, D., El-Assady, M., Keim, D. A., & Chen, M. (2021). Why visualize? Arguments for visual support in decision making. IEEE Computer Graphics and Applications, 41(2), 17–22.

From this information, a manager can derive the following managerial insight:

"Profit margins are consistently higher when testing is performed by more experienced QA technicians."

However, insight alone is not enough. In managerial settings, insight should support a decision or recommendation.1Berinato, S. (2016). Visualizations that really work. Harvard Business Review.6Nussbaumer Knaflic, C. (2015). Storytelling with data: A data visualization guide for business professionals. Wiley.

A straightforward recommendation based on this insight might be: "Assign experienced QA technicians (levels III–IV) to testing activities across all drone models."

At first glance, this recommendation appears reasonable. But as discussed in this module, effective managerial communication also requires attention to context. The same insight can support different actions depending on the organization’s circumstances.1Berinato, S. (2016). Visualizations that really work. Harvard Business Review.

For example, the appropriate recommendation might differ in the following situations:

  • If the organization faces high turnover or a competitive labor market, the priority may be to implement a retention strategy for experienced QA technicians.
  • If the company is expanding and hiring new staff, the recommendation may be to prioritize candidates with prior QA experience in drone testing.
  • If the organization is focused on long-term capability development, a better recommendation may be to invest in training programs that help junior QA technicians build expertise more quickly.

This example highlights a central idea in managerial data communication: visualization helps reveal patterns, but managers must interpret those patterns within context and translate them into appropriate actions.5Midway, S. R. (2020). Principles of effective data visualization. Patterns, 1(9), 100141.8Streeb, D., El-Assady, M., Keim, D. A., & Chen, M. (2021). Why visualize? Arguments for visual support in decision making. IEEE Computer Graphics and Applications, 41(2), 17–22.

In practice, the most effective decisions are not based on data alone, or even on insight alone, but on the ability to connect data → information → insight → recommendation in a way that fits the organization’s goals and constraints.10Tukey, J. W., & Wilk, M. B. (1966). Data analysis and statistics: An expository overview. In Proceedings of the November 7–10, 1966, fall joint computer conference (pp. 695–709).

Key Takeaways

Several key principles emerge from this introduction to data visualization in managerial decision making:

  • Visualization is not primarily about aesthetics; it is about clarity and insight.
  • Managers do not need more data; they need interpretations that support decisions.
  • Effective visualizations reduce cognitive load and guide attention toward meaningful patterns.
  • Evidence-based management relies on communicating data in ways that decision makers can interpret quickly.
  • The most effective visualizations are embedded in a broader narrative that connects data, insight, and recommendation.

In the modules that follow, we will examine specific visualization principles, chart types, and communication strategies that help managers transform complex data into clear and actionable insights.

Managerial Insight

Managers rarely have the time to review raw datasets or complex analytical reports. Instead, they rely on clear visual summaries that allow them to quickly identify patterns, risks, and opportunities. Effective data visualization helps managers move from data to insight more efficiently, enabling faster and more informed decision making.

However, poorly designed visuals can have the opposite effect. When charts are confusing or misleading, managers may misinterpret the evidence or ignore the analysis altogether. As a result, one of the most valuable skills for managers today is the ability to evaluate and communicate data visually, ensuring that decisions are based on clear and accurate interpretations of the evidence.
