Module 3: Choosing the Right Visualization

Introduction

Managers frequently encounter charts in dashboards, reports, and presentations. However, not all charts communicate information effectively. A poorly chosen visualization can obscure patterns, exaggerate relationships, or make comparisons difficult.3Few, S. (2012). Show me the numbers: Designing tables and graphs to enlighten (2nd ed.). Analytics Press.6Cairo, A. (2016). The truthful art: Data, charts, and maps for communication. New Riders.

Choosing the right visualization is therefore an important managerial communication skill. Visualization design is not only a technical decision made by analysts; it is also a strategic decision that influences how managers interpret evidence and make decisions.

Different types of data require different visual structures.1Financial Times. (n.d.). Visual Vocabulary. https://ft-interactive.github.io/visual-vocabulary/ A chart that works well for comparing categories may fail when used to show trends over time. Similarly, charts that display proportions may be ineffective when the goal is to identify precise differences between values.

This module introduces a framework that helps managers evaluate whether a visualization is appropriate for the question being asked. By the end of the module, students should be able to assess visualization choices not only in technical terms but also in terms of their clarity, accuracy, and usefulness for managerial decision making.

Why Visualization Choice Matters

Human perception is highly sensitive to specific visual cues, such as length, position, and direction. According to the theory of graphical perception, people interpret some visual encodings far more accurately than others.2Cleveland, W. S., & McGill, R. (1984). Graphical perception: Theory, experimentation, and application to the development of graphical methods. Journal of the American Statistical Association, 79(387), 531–554. Their research established a hierarchy of accuracy:

  • Position along a common scale (e.g., bar charts, scatter plots) is the most accurately perceived; people can compare bar lengths easily.
  • They are less accurate when comparing angles (as in pie charts).2Cleveland, W. S., & McGill, R. (1984). Graphical perception: Theory, experimentation, and application to the development of graphical methods. Journal of the American Statistical Association, 79(387), 531–554.
  • Area, Volume, and Color Saturation are the least accurate cues for perceiving quantitative differences; people struggle even more when comparing areas and volumes.2Cleveland, W. S., & McGill, R. (1984). Graphical perception: Theory, experimentation, and application to the development of graphical methods. Journal of the American Statistical Association, 79(387), 531–554.

As a result, the same dataset can appear clearer or more confusing depending on the chart type used. For example, viewers can compare bar lengths easily, but they are significantly less accurate when comparing angles in a pie chart or the areas of bubbles in a map.3Few, S. (2012). Show me the numbers: Designing tables and graphs to enlighten (2nd ed.). Analytics Press. Stephen Few notes that effective visualizations allow viewers to perceive patterns and relationships quickly through preattentive processing, avoiding unnecessary cognitive load. For managers making decisions under time pressure, this clarity is essential to avoid being misled by low-accuracy encodings like 3D volumes or complex color gradients.5Knaflic, C. N. (2015). Storytelling with data: A data visualization guide for business professionals. Wiley. Understanding how different visualization types communicate information helps managers determine whether the evidence presented in reports or dashboards is easy to interpret or potentially misleading.

Types of Analytical Questions

Most managerial analyses involve a small number of common analytical questions. The Financial Times Visual Vocabulary1Financial Times. (n.d.). Visual Vocabulary. https://ft-interactive.github.io/visual-vocabulary/ and the research by Cleveland and McGill2Cleveland, W. S., & McGill, R. (1984). Graphical perception: Theory, experimentation, and application to the development of graphical methods. Journal of the American Statistical Association, 79(387), 531–554. offer clear guidance for choosing the right "visual language" by matching the task to the most accurate perceptual cue. The most common analytical tasks include:

  • Comparisons. Showing magnitude between categories. These should prioritize position along a common scale (e.g., bar charts) for maximum accuracy
  • Time series (trends). Showing how a variable changes over time. Line charts are the standard because they leverage direction and position.
  • Distributions. Showing the frequency or spread of data. Histograms or box plots are effective for showing density.
  • Relationships. Showing correlations between variables. Scatter plots are the "gold standard" here as they rely on 2D position
  • Composition. Showing how a total is broken down into parts. While pie charts are common, stacked bars are often preferred because they allow for comparison along a linear scale rather than an angle.

Each task requires a different visual structure.

Comparisons

Comparison charts answer questions such as:

  • Which department has the highest performance?
  • How do sales differ across product categories?
  • Which region generated the most revenue?

These questions require viewers to compare values across categories. The most effective charts for comparisons include:

  • Bar charts
  • Column charts
  • Dot plots

Bar charts are particularly effective because humans are very good at comparing length along a common axis.2Cleveland, W. S., & McGill, R. (1984). Graphical perception: Theory, experimentation, and application to the development of graphical methods. Journal of the American Statistical Association, 79(387), 531–554. For example, a bar chart comparing quarterly revenue across regions allows managers to quickly identify the highest-performing market. In contrast, using a pie chart to show the same data would make comparisons more difficult because viewers must compare angles rather than lengths.2Cleveland, W. S., & McGill, R. (1984). Graphical perception: Theory, experimentation, and application to the development of graphical methods. Journal of the American Statistical Association, 79(387), 531–554.3Few, S. (2012). Show me the numbers: Designing tables and graphs to enlighten (2nd ed.). Analytics Press.

Note: Vertical and horizontal bar charts are useful for comparing values across categories, such as which region generated the most revenue or which department has the highest performance.

Trends

Trend charts show how a variable changes over time. Typical managerial questions include:

  • Are sales increasing or declining?
  • How has customer acquisition changed over the past year?
  • What patterns appear in monthly demand?

The most common visualization for trends is the line chart. Line charts work well because they show continuity over time, allowing viewers to quickly identify patterns such as:

  • upward trends
  • downward trends
  • seasonality
  • sudden changes

For example, a line chart showing monthly sales revenue across two years can reveal seasonal patterns that might not be visible in a table of numbers.

Edward Tufte emphasizes that time-series data is often best communicated through line charts because they preserve the temporal structure of the data.4Tufte, E. R. (2001). The visual display of quantitative information (2nd ed.). Graphics Press.

Note: Trend charts are especially useful when the managerial question concerns direction over time, such as whether a rate is rising or falling, whether seasonality is present, or whether a surplus or deficit is widening.

Distributions

Distribution charts help managers understand how values are spread across a dataset. These charts answer questions such as:

  • Are most customers clustered within a narrow price range?
  • Is employee performance evenly distributed or concentrated?
  • Are there outliers in operational metrics?

Common visualizations for distributions include:

  • Histograms
  • Box plots
  • Density plots

For example, a histogram showing customer purchase amounts may reveal that most customers spend within a moderate range, while a small number of high-spending customers form a long tail in the distribution. Understanding distributions can help managers identify risk, variability, and unusual cases.7Healy, K. (2018). Data visualization: A practical introduction. Princeton University Press.

Note: Histograms, density plots and box plots are best used for demonstrating how values are spread across a dataset; they are particularly helpful for identifying risks and outliers.

Relationships

Relationship charts help decision makers examine whether two variables are associated with one another. Typical managerial questions include:

  • Does marketing spending correlate with revenue growth?
  • Is customer satisfaction related to retention?
  • Are employee training hours associated with productivity?

The most common visualization used to explore relationships is the scatter plot.1Financial Times. (n.d.). Visual Vocabulary. https://ft-interactive.github.io/visual-vocabulary/7Healy, K. (2018). Data visualization: A practical introduction. Princeton University Press. Scatter plots display pairs of observations and allow viewers to identify patterns such as:7Healy, K. (2018). Data visualization: A practical introduction. Princeton University Press.

  • positive relationships
  • negative relationships
  • clusters
  • outliers

For example, a scatter plot comparing advertising spending and sales across markets may reveal whether higher marketing investment tends to produce greater revenue. For managers, such visualizations can highlight potential relationships that may require further investigation before strategic decisions are made.

Note: The first scatterplot shows that this type of chart can reveal simple linear relationships. However, as you can see in the three other charts, real scatter plots usually contain variation. Positive and negative relationships are rarely perfect lines, clusters appear as dense groups of observations, and outliers are points that stand apart from the main pattern.

Composition

Composition charts show how a total is divided among different components.1Financial Times. (n.d.). Visual Vocabulary. https://ft-interactive.github.io/visual-vocabulary/ These charts answer questions such as:

  • What share of total revenue comes from each product line?
  • How is the marketing budget allocated across channels?
  • How has market share changed over time?

Common charts for composition include:

  • Stacked bar charts
  • Stacked area charts
  • Pie charts

Although pie charts are widely used, they are often less effective than stacked bar charts because it is difficult to compare angles precisely.2Cleveland, W. S., & McGill, R. (1984). Graphical perception: Theory, experimentation, and application to the development of graphical methods. Journal of the American Statistical Association, 79(387), 531–554.3Few, S. (2012). Show me the numbers: Designing tables and graphs to enlighten (2nd ed.). Analytics Press. As visualization experts frequently note, pie charts are most appropriate when there are only a few categories and the goal is simply to show proportions.

When Common Charts Fail

Even commonly used charts can fail when applied incorrectly.6Cairo, A. (2016). The truthful art: Data, charts, and maps for communication. New Riders. One frequent mistake involves using pie charts to compare too many categories. When a pie chart contains many slices, it becomes difficult to interpret relative proportions. Another common problem occurs when line charts are used for categorical data rather than time-series data. Because line charts imply continuity, using them for categories such as departments or product types can create misleading interpretations.4Tufte, E. R. (2001). The visual display of quantitative information (2nd ed.). Graphics Press. Similarly, stacked charts may obscure comparisons between categories because only the bottom segment shares a common baseline.3Few, S. (2012). Show me the numbers: Designing tables and graphs to enlighten (2nd ed.). Analytics Press.7Healy, K. (2018). Data visualization: A practical introduction. Princeton University Press.

These issues highlight an important principle of visualization: The chart type should match the analytical question.

Managerial Justification of Visualization Choices

In managerial settings, charts are often presented in dashboards, reports, or presentations prepared by analysts. Managers may not always create these visualizations themselves, but they must evaluate whether the chosen visualization clearly communicates the evidence needed for decision making.5Knaflic, C. N. (2015). Storytelling with data: A data visualization guide for business professionals. Wiley.6Cairo, A. (2016). The truthful art: Data, charts, and maps for communication. New Riders.

Rather than focusing only on technical conventions, visualization choices should be justified in terms of decision relevance and interpretability. For example:

  • A bar chart may be used because it allows managers to quickly compare sales across regions.
  • A line chart may be selected because it clearly reveals seasonal patterns in demand.
  • A scatter plot may be used to illustrate a potential relationship between customer satisfaction and retention.

By framing visualization choices in terms of clarity, accuracy, and decision usefulness, organizations can ensure that charts support effective communication rather than simply displaying data.3Few, S. (2012). Show me the numbers: Designing tables and graphs to enlighten (2nd ed.). Analytics Press.6Cairo, A. (2016). The truthful art: Data, charts, and maps for communication. New Riders. Managers who understand these principles are better equipped to evaluate the visualizations presented to them and ensure that decisions are based on clear and interpretable evidence.

Practical Example

Suppose a manager wants to understand whether customer satisfaction is related to customer retention. The managerial question is not “What percentage of customers fall into each satisfaction category?” but rather “Do higher satisfaction scores tend to correspond with higher retention rates?” That difference matters because the analytical task is to examine a relationship, not a composition.1Financial Times. (n.d.). Visual Vocabulary. https://ft-interactive.github.io/visual-vocabulary/7Healy, K. (2018). Data visualization: A practical introduction. Princeton University Press.

Several chart types could be used to display the same general topic, but only some support the decision clearly. A pie chart may show how customers are distributed across satisfaction groups, but it cannot show whether satisfaction and retention move together. A scatter plot, by contrast, places each customer segment or market as a point, with satisfaction on one axis and retention on the other. This allows the manager to see whether the relationship appears positive, negative, weak, or inconsistent.2Cleveland, W. S., & McGill, R. (1984). Graphical perception: Theory, experimentation, and application to the development of graphical methods. Journal of the American Statistical Association, 79(387), 531–554.7Healy, K. (2018). Data visualization: A practical introduction. Princeton University Press.

Key Takeaways

  • Effective visualization begins with understanding the analytical question.1Financial Times. (n.d.). Visual Vocabulary. https://ft-interactive.github.io/visual-vocabulary/
  • Different chart types are designed to answer different questions, such as comparisons, trends, distributions, relationships, and composition.
  • Selecting the appropriate visualization improves clarity, reduces cognitive effort, and allows managers to interpret evidence quickly when evaluating reports, dashboards, and presentations.
  • Poor chart selection, in contrast, can obscure patterns and lead to misinterpretation.6Cairo, A. (2016). The truthful art: Data, charts, and maps for communication. New Riders.
  • Managers who understand visualization principles are better equipped to evaluate reports, dashboards, and presentations that rely on data.

Managerial Insight

Different managerial questions require different types of visualizations. A chart that effectively shows a trend over time may be poorly suited for comparing categories or identifying relationships between variables.

Managers frequently rely on dashboards, reports, and presentations that summarize complex data across multiple areas of the organization. Understanding how different visualization types communicate information helps managers ask better questions about the evidence presented.

For example, if a dashboard uses a pie chart to compare many categories, the relative differences may be difficult to interpret. A bar chart might communicate the same information more clearly. Managers who understand these differences can ensure that the visualizations used in their organizations accurately support decision making.3Few, S. (2012). Show me the numbers: Designing tables and graphs to enlighten (2nd ed.). Analytics Press.6Cairo, A. (2016). The truthful art: Data, charts, and maps for communication. New Riders.
